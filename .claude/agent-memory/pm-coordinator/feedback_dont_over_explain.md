# Write to members like they already know how this works

Todd, 2026-09-11, on a flex email that closed with *"Your balance is on your
account page — it carries over, so there's no rush to spend it all at once"*:

> "These people should know how to use the portal by now so the last sentence is
> sort of dumb"

**Why:** the flex/store-credit list is Todd's most engaged segment — every one of
the 41 has money on account and 18 topped up that month. Explaining where their
balance lives, or how ordering works, talks down to people who have been doing it
all season. It also pads an email whose whole value is speed: what's new, what's
gone, when it closes.

**How to apply:**
- No tutorials, no "here's how to find X," no reassurance about mechanics, for any
  audience that has used the portal before. Assume competence.
- Onboarding language belongs in onboarding emails only — genuinely new members.
- The useful content of a weekly list email is: **what's new, what's gone, the
  deadline, the link.** Everything else is filler.
- Same principle as the earlier note that emails had gone *"kurt and impersonal"* —
  warm is good, padding is not. Warmth comes from voice, not from extra sentences.

**Related:** [[flex-weekly-cadence]] for who this audience is,
[[never-send-without-confirmation]] for the gate that should have let Todd catch
this line before it went.

## Order confirmations: confirm the ITEMS, not the money

Todd, 2026-09-14: *"No need to have the totals in the email. Just confirm what
they asked."*

A confirmation exists to tell a chef their order was heard correctly. Restating
prices and a total turns a two-line note into a quote, implies they might not
know what they agreed to, and invites a pricing conversation that wasn't
happening. **The invoice carries the money.** Confirmations carry the items.

Exception: when the price itself is the news — a volume tier they haven't seen,
a substitution at a different price, or a number they explicitly asked for.
