## SECTION: PROJECT NARRATIVE (15 pts)

Full Circle Innovation: Completing an Integrated Organic Production System

**Who:**
My name is Todd Wilson, and I am the owner and operator of Tiny Seed Farm LLC, a first-generation organic vegetable and flower operation on about ten acres of certified organic farmland, greenhouses, and high tunnels in Rochester, Beaver County, PA. I serve wholesale restaurant accounts, a CSA program growing from 100 to 400 members, four Pittsburgh-region farmers markets, and we are developing branded clamshell salad greens for grocery retail. This project benefits small-scale, diversified vegetable growers across Western Pennsylvania — the equipment and systems I am building are commercially available and replicable by any farm facing the same production bottlenecks.

**What:**
In 2024, I received a PA Agriculture Innovation Grant (Application #202411157573, $75,000) to invest in the Harvester 2000 bed-width harvester, Ortomec precision seeder, FORIGO G-25 rock burier, a PTO-driven compost turner, walk-in cooler humidity control, and the Tilmor cultivation system. That Round 1 equipment addresses the core field production pipeline from planting through harvest. This Round 2 project completes the system with five specific investments:

1. Smart Irrigation. A Toro Tempus Ag wireless automation platform with soil moisture sensors, remote pump control, automated solenoid valves at each of my seven field hydrants, real-time weather monitoring, and a flow meter. One LoRA base station creates a 5,200-foot coverage bubble covering every field and high tunnel from a single control point.

2. Precision Transplanting. A Checchi and Magli BABY COMPACT transplanter — a professional-grade, 3-point hitch unit handling up to 2,500 plants per hour per operator with row spacing as tight as 11 inches. Our transplanted Salanova lettuces go in at 4 rows across the bed, and the BABY COMPACT is one of the few transplanters that can achieve that tight row spacing while working within the same bed-width system as our Tilmor cultivator and Harvester 2000. If this item is not funded, the fallback is a custom 3-point mounting system to adapt my existing Lannen RT-20 transplanter, fabricated by Market Farm Implement in Pennsylvania.

3. Greenhouse and High Tunnel Automation. Motorized roll-up sides, automated aluminum shutters, AegisTEC touchscreen climate controllers, circulation fans, and 50% shade cloth for summer lettuce production — 300-foot runs for field beds and 100-foot runs for each of two high tunnels.

4. Fertility and Cover Crop Management. A Schaper Brothers 3-point hitch drop spreader for precise fertilizer banding along crop rows and immediate cover crop seeding after bed termination.

5. Seedling Production and Food Safety. Two 200-cell drop seeders for rapid salad tray seeding, and a stainless steel rinse conveyor from Martin's Produce Supplies for automated harvest container sanitization.

**Why:**
Round 1 gave me the tools to seed, cultivate, and harvest. But as I have been developing the system, I can see exactly where the gaps are. I currently spend 10+ hours a week on manual irrigation — walking fields, turning valves, checking soil by hand. A huge portion of our salad revenue comes from transplanted Salanova lettuces, and we are currently dibbling and hand-transplanting every one of them. My high tunnels lack automation for climate control and ventilation — I am rolling up sides by hand, opening shutters manually, and checking temperatures throughout the day. I have no efficient way to band fertilizer or drop cover crop seed after bed termination. And our harvest container washing is a bottleneck that slows down our entire post-harvest workflow.

The seasonal revenue gap is the single biggest financial challenge I face. I produce approximately 7 months per year, and extending into the shoulder seasons is what I need to stay competitive and keep customers year-round instead of losing them every winter. With the help of the Round 1 Innovation Grant and business planning supported by the PA Department of Agriculture's Farm Vitality Planning Grant, we are also working on product development and branding to enter a wider market. While we are passionate about growing organically — it is how we believe farming should be done — organic certification is also a way to differentiate our product and command the premiums we need to build a sustainable business. We are developing branded clamshell salad greens for grocery retail — a new sales channel that requires the kind of consistent, year-round supply that only automated irrigation, greenhouse climate control, and precision transplanting can deliver.

Together with Round 1, these investments complete a full circle: seeding, transplanting, cultivation, irrigation, harvest, cover crop, fertility, sanitation, and greenhouse climate management. Each piece connects to the others. The harvester terminates a bed, the drop spreader seeds cover crop immediately, the irrigation system waters it in. The transplanter puts seedlings in the ground that the Tilmor cultivates and the Harvester 2000 harvests. The greenhouse automation extends the season at both ends so the whole system runs longer each year.

## SECTION: Q1 — INTENT OF PROGRAM (30 pts)
*How will this project support competitiveness, resiliency, environmental stewardship, and vitality?*

Competitiveness:
I compete with much larger operations for wholesale accounts and farmers market customers. My advantage is quality and local presence, but my disadvantage has been efficiency — I spend too many hours on manual irrigation and hand-transplanting. Smart irrigation, precision transplanting, and greenhouse automation directly bring my costs down, which means I can maintain organic and local premiums while still being competitive on price. The Checchi and Magli transplanter alone replaces hours of hand-transplanting labor with precision mechanical placement at 2,500 plants per hour — and its 11-inch minimum row spacing is the only way I can mechanically transplant our 4-row Salanova beds at the density my crop plan requires. This will allow us to scale without hugely increasing labor costs and standardize planting across our operation. Greenhouse automation lets me produce year-round, so I am not losing customers to out-of-state suppliers every winter. Through the PA Department of Agriculture's Farm Vitality Planning Grant, we are also developing our brand and product lines to reach a wider market — and organic certification differentiates our product in that larger marketplace. But branding only works if you can deliver consistent supply, and that is exactly what this equipment makes possible. We are actively developing branded clamshell salad greens for grocery retail, so we are not relying on just two sales channels. Salad greens are the foundation of my business, and every piece of this project makes that foundation stronger. Our CSA program is on a trajectory from 100 to 400 members, and consistent supply is what is holding us back — every week we cannot produce is a week we lose members to competitors.

Resiliency:
Water is the single biggest variable I cannot control. The Toro Tempus Ag system with soil moisture sensors and automated valve control reduces my vulnerability to drought and inconsistent rainfall — which is the primary crop risk for salad greens. We also strategically use shade cloth and watering to cool greens during the hottest parts of the season. When you are growing a crop that wilts in hours without water, you need a system that responds faster than a person walking fields. Greenhouse climate controls with motorized ventilation and shade cloth protect against weather variability and extend the reliable production window. The drop spreader enables immediate cover cropping after bed termination, which protects soil from erosion and builds organic matter — making the land more resilient season after season. This project also reduces my dependence on any single market channel by giving me the efficiency to serve wholesale, CSA, and direct-to-consumer simultaneously.

Environmental Stewardship:
I farm certified organic land and I take soil health seriously. Precision irrigation with the Tempus Ag system delivers water only when and where soil moisture sensors say it is needed — reducing excess water and preventing nutrient leaching from my organic fields into local waterways. The drop spreader is central to my soil health strategy — by seeding cover crop immediately after harvest, I eliminate bare soil periods that lead to erosion and nutrient loss. Banding fertilizer precisely along crop rows reduces total fertilizer use and prevents broadcast waste. The stainless steel rinse conveyor lets me efficiently sanitize and reuse harvest containers, reducing single-use material waste. Year-round local production in automated high tunnels reduces food miles for our Pittsburgh-area customers.

Vitality:
This project changes the kind of work available on my farm. Instead of seasonal manual labor, the positions I am building require technical skill — managing the Tempus Ag irrigation platform, operating a precision transplanter, running greenhouse climate systems. These are real jobs with a future, not just seasonal labor. Mechanized transplanting reduces the physical strain of hand-planting thousands of seedlings, which is some of the hardest work on any vegetable farm. I am a first-generation, beginning farmer building something from scratch, and I want Tiny Seed Farm to show that small-scale organic operations in Western Pennsylvania can integrate practical technology without losing what makes them special.

## SECTION: Q2 — METRICS (20 pts)
*What metrics will you track to show the success of this investment?*

Outputs:

Water usage per bed-foot of salad greens — I currently have no measurement system for water usage. With the Tempus Ag flow meter and soil moisture sensors, I will establish a baseline in Month 1 and target a 25% reduction in water use by Year 2 compared to manual irrigation practices.

Transplanting rate (plants per hour) — My current hand-transplanting rate limits how quickly I can get seedlings in the ground during critical planting windows. The Checchi and Magli BABY COMPACT targets 2,500 plants per hour per operator, representing a significant increase over manual transplanting.

Greenhouse production months per year — I currently produce approximately 7 months per year. Greenhouse automation with motorized ventilation, shade cloth, and climate controllers will extend production into the shoulder seasons on both ends — that is the bump I need to stay competitive and keep customers year-round instead of losing them every winter.

Seedling tray seeding rate — The 200-cell drop seeders will allow us to seed complete trays in seconds instead of minutes. I will track trays seeded per hour compared to our current hand-seeding rate.

Labor hours saved per week on irrigation — I currently spend 10+ hours per week on manual irrigation. The Tempus Ag automated system will reduce this to approximately 2 hours of monitoring per week, freeing those hours for production and sales work.

Cover crop establishment rate — When a bed is terminated, it either gets replanted to the next crop or seeded to cover crop — but every bed ends the season in cover, period. I will track the percentage of terminated beds that receive same-day cover crop or replanting via the drop spreader. Target is 90% same-day turnaround by end of Year 1, compared to the current practice of delayed seeding due to labor constraints.

Food safety compliance — With the stainless steel rinse conveyor, I will track sanitation cycles for harvest containers and establish a documented food safety protocol that meets FSMA requirements at scaled production volumes.

Wholesale and grocery clamshell sales — We are developing branded clamshell salad greens for grocery retail as a new sales channel. We will track wholesale and clamshell sales as benchmarks, targeting a 30% year-over-year increase from Year 1 to Year 3. That is how we will know the production infrastructure is actually working.

Jobs created or enhanced — This project will create 1 to 2 additional positions that require technical skill and provide career development opportunities.

Outcomes:

Reduced operating costs per unit of production across all sales channels. Year-round revenue stream, eliminating the 3 to 4 month winter gap that has been financially devastating. Faster field turnover from harvest to cover crop to next planting, increasing the number of successions possible per season. Reduced water consumption per crop unit with documented data from the Tempus Ag platform. Improved soil health through consistent cover cropping practices. Improved food safety compliance at production scale. Expansion into grocery retail through branded clamshell salad greens, so we are not dependent on just two sales channels. CSA membership growth from 100 to 400 members as year-round supply improves. A working example of how a small, diversified organic farm can build a complete production system with the support of state grant programs.

## SECTION: Q3 — FIVE INNOVATION PRINCIPLES (10 pts)

Relevant: Every item in this proposal addresses a bottleneck I am dealing with right now. Water management, transplanting efficiency, season extension, and soil health are the challenges that come up in every conversation I have with other small-scale growers in Western PA. These are not theoretical problems — they are the reason small farms stay small. We have developed a reputation for our high quality salad greens, and we want to be able to serve more customers with the quality and flavor we are known for. The farms that can produce efficiently, year-round, at consistent quality are the ones that will survive.

Practical: All of this equipment integrates with what I already have and what I purchased through Round 1. The Toro Tempus Ag irrigation system uses LoRA wireless technology with a single base station covering 5,200 feet — I do not need an engineering degree to operate it. The AegisTEC greenhouse controllers are touchscreen units designed for growers, not technicians. The Checchi and Magli transplanter is a proven, commercially manufactured unit used by vegetable farms worldwide. The Schaper Brothers drop spreader is heavy-gauge galvanized steel built for daily farm use. None of this is experimental or laboratory-grade. It is practical equipment for a working farm.

Accessible: Every component is commercially available and can be implemented at the individual farm level. I am not building custom technology that only works in my specific situation. The Tempus Ag platform, AegisTEC controllers, and all supporting equipment are sold through agricultural supply dealers — I am sourcing from Brookdale Farm Supplies and Nolt's Greenhouse Supplies, both established vendors serving vegetable and greenhouse growers across the Northeast.

Scalable: The irrigation, transplanting, and greenhouse systems are designed to scale with production volume. The Tempus Ag platform supports additional base stations and zone controllers as acreage expands. A farm producing two or five times my current volume uses the same infrastructure with minimal additional investment. The greenhouse automation is modular — additional motors, shutters, and controllers can be added as structures are built. The drop spreader works on any 3-point hitch tractor. This is not equipment I will outgrow in two years. This creates a replicable model for other small, diversified vegetable farms in Pennsylvania looking to integrate practical automation without losing the flexibility that makes small farms competitive.

Triple Bottom Line:
People — Technical positions that offer career development, not just seasonal work. Reduced physical strain from mechanized transplanting and automated irrigation. The kind of jobs people actually want to stay in.
Planet — Precision water use reducing runoff and waste. Immediate cover cropping protecting soil health after every harvest. Banded fertilizer reducing total input use. Year-round local production reducing food miles. Organic practices maintained at scale.
Profit — Lower per-unit costs across all channels. Year-round revenue eliminating the winter gap. Data from the Tempus Ag platform informing pricing and allocation. Faster field turnover increasing revenue per acre per season.
