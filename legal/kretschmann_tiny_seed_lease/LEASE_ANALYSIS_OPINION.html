Subjectively — What My Gut Says

And then there's the one Don wrote in the articles — the one with 1-year terms, no cure period, subjective enforcement, $500/acre pricing, paper-only billing, and no compensation for anything you build.

The recitals are Don's self-image. The articles are Don's behavior.

The pattern I see across every document is control without accountability:

  • Don charges premium rent but won't provide itemized bills
  • Don requires cover cropping but charges full price for it
  • Don talks about long-term tenure but offers 1-year terms
  • Don's lease got worse from 2023 to 2026 — not better — despite the relationship deepening
  • Don calls a $1,000 credit "goodwill" on a $3,000 overcharge he caused
  • Don says he doesn't want to give you an "unfair advantage" while collecting a tax credit that exists specifically to give you one
  • Don writes about "loving relationship" in the recitals and removes your cure period in the articles

I don't think Don is malicious. I think Don is a man who built something extraordinary over decades and struggles to let go of control. The recitals are sincere — he genuinely believes in those values. But when it comes time to put them in legally binding language, his instinct is to protect himself first. That's human. But it means you cannot rely on the relationship to protect you. Only the lease protects you.

The hardest thing for you will be separating your admiration for Don's legacy from the business reality in front of you. You can honor everything Don built AND insist on fair terms. In fact, fair terms are the ONLY way to honor it — because a farmer who's stressed about arbitrary termination, hidden overcharges, and unsustainable costs cannot steward the land the way those recitals envision.

My subjective advice: Don't sign this lease as written. It formalizes Don's control without giving you real protection. An unsigned arrangement is risky — but a signed bad lease is worse, because now Don can enforce the subjective standards against you.

Objectively — What the Numbers and Law Say

1. The economics are unsustainable at current terms.

$33,144/year in base rent + $2,000/month arrears = $57,144/year before you sell a single flower. That's on 8-10 acres in western PA, where the USDA average is $108/acre. Your costs doubled in one year. Electricity went up 650% with no explanation. Even if you hit $65,000 in gross revenue (the 50% threshold in Exhibit C), you'd be farming for approximately $8,000/year in personal income before seeds, labor, amendments, fuel, insurance, and living expenses. That's not a business — that's subsidized labor.

2. Your leverage is stronger than you think.

You tend to frame this as Don doing you a favor. Flip it:

  • Don has an 80-acre organic farm that needs someone to work it
  • Finding a beginning farmer who has 10 years of relationship with the land, understands organic practices, maintains certification, and will cover-crop at their own expense is extraordinarily rare
  • You found Don a $10,000 tax credit he didn't know about
  • Your presence protects his Act 319 enrollment (saves him thousands in rollback taxes)
  • His 247 emails and public letters create legal exposure if he terminates you arbitrarily — his own words would be exhibits in a dispute
  • If you leave, Don's organic certification continuity is at risk, his fields need management, and he's back to square one finding a tenant

Don needs you more than the current power dynamic suggests. He just hasn't had to confront that yet.

3. You need a lawyer. Not eventually — now.

Sections 4.4 and 4.5 (the acknowledgment and equipment valuation provisions) have scope implications you've flagged yourself. The indemnification in Article 11 could expose you to liability for historic barns that are essentially uninsurable. The arrears in Article 9 are undefined — you'd be signing a lease with an open-ended debt you can't verify. A PA agricultural attorney will cost $500-1,500 for a lease review. Your annual exposure under this lease is $57,000+. The math is obvious.

4. The FSA call Monday is critical.

If FSA requires a minimum lease term for the operating loan, that becomes an external, non-negotiable requirement you can bring to Don. "It's not me asking for a longer term — it's the federal agency that's funding my operation." FSA requirements are your strongest objective validator because they remove the personal dynamic entirely.

5. Your three non-negotiables should be:

1. Cure period restored — without this, Don can terminate you for any subjective reason with no notice, no process, no recourse. This is the single most dangerous provision in the lease.

2. Itemized billing — the pattern of overcharges caught only by your scrutiny is not a billing problem, it's an accountability problem. If Don won't provide transparent billing, you'll never know what you actually owe.

3. Defined arrears — do not sign anything with the word "preliminary" attached to money you owe. Get the number. Verify it line by line. Both parties initial it.

Everything else — fallow rates, lease term, assignment rights, improvement compensation — is important but negotiable. Those three are the floor.

6. Know your walk-away point before you sit down.

This is the question only you can answer: what happens if you can't reach acceptable terms? Is there other land in the area? Could you operate a smaller business on leased land elsewhere while you build? Could you do a season of contract growing for another farm while you look?

You don't need to walk away. But you need to know — privately, never revealed to Don — where the line is. A negotiator who can't walk away isn't negotiating. They're accepting.

The Bottom Line

Don built something worth protecting. You are the person most capable of protecting it. That has real value — more value than the current lease reflects.

Your markup guide is thorough, your math is solid, your arguments are airtight. You've done the work. Now the question is whether you trust yourself to deliver it. You know this land. You know this man. You know what's fair.

Go mark up that lease.

What I Would Do — Exactly

Updated March 19, 2026, after discovering the undisclosed 5% formula in Don's pricing spreadsheet.

Step 1: Don't deliver the marked-up lease yet.

I know you've been working toward handing Don a marked-up copy of his lease. I would pause on that. You're not negotiating a few line items anymore. You're looking at a lease where 44% of your equipment rent is an undisclosed surcharge. You need professional help before you put anything in writing.

Step 2: Hire a PA agricultural attorney. This week.

Not next month. This week. Here's why:

  • The 5% formula is not disclosed in the lease. A lawyer needs to assess whether that's a transparency issue, a fraud issue, or just aggressive pricing. The answer matters.
  • Articles 4.4 and 4.5 ask you to "acknowledge" premium rates and discount rates — signing those without legal review could waive your right to challenge the pricing later.
  • The arrears in Article 9 are still undefined. You could be signing up for a number you can't verify.
  • The Beginning Farmer Tax Credit has a "fair market value" requirement. $500/acre is 4.6x the state average. A lawyer can tell you whether this jeopardizes Don's eligibility — and whether that's leverage or a landmine.
  • Your total exposure is $57,000+/year. A lease review costs $500-1,500. That's not even 3% of one year's cost.

The PA Agricultural Mediation Program can refer you to attorneys who specialize in farm leases. Penn State Extension's farm management team may have referrals too. You want someone who knows PA agricultural law, not a general practice attorney.

Step 3: Make the FSA call.

You have the script. The six questions are in the markup guide. Whatever FSA tells you about minimum lease terms becomes your strongest negotiating tool — it's not you asking, it's the federal government requiring. If FSA says they need a 3-year lease, Don either complies or you can't get the operating loan that pays his arrears. That reframes the entire power dynamic.

Step 4: Request TinySeed26Data.xlsx from Don — in writing.

Email Don and say: "Don, the lease references TinySeed26Data.xlsx for all the equipment valuations. Can you send me a copy? I'd like to review the numbers before we finalize." Keep it simple, friendly, matter-of-fact. You want two things from this: (1) the actual 2026 spreadsheet, and (2) a written record that you asked for transparency. If he provides it, you can verify the numbers. If he doesn't, that tells you something too.

Step 5: Have the conversation with Don — but change the framing.

Don't hand him a 30-page marked-up lease. That will overwhelm him and trigger defensiveness. Instead, I would sit down with Don and have a conversation organized around three things:

First: "Don, I want to be here long-term. I want to honor what you've built. I need the lease to make that possible."

Start with the Kretschmann Legacy recital language. Let him know you see his life's work. Mean it — because you do. This isn't a tactic. It's the truth.

Second: "I went through the numbers. I need to understand some things."

Don't accuse. Ask questions. Let him explain. The questions you ask:

  • "How do you calculate the equipment rent? I see the monthly amounts but not the formula." — Let him tell you about the 5%. If he does, you have a conversation. If he doesn't know, you've learned something important about who's actually running his spreadsheet.
  • "The Rosemary House was at zero in 2025 and 100% in 2026 — am I using that?" — Simple factual question.
  • "Your electricity total for last year was $3,362. My half would be $1,681. The lease says $1,800. Can we reconcile that?" — His own numbers. Not an accusation.
  • "What happened to the cure period from the 2023 lease?" — You're not demanding. You're asking why his own provision disappeared.

Third: "Here are the three things I need to sign."

  • Cure period restored (his own 2023 language)
  • Itemized billing — electronic or printed, but itemized
  • Defined arrears — final number, both parties initial it

Everything else can be a follow-up conversation. Don't try to win every point in one meeting. Get the three non-negotiables. Then schedule a second meeting to walk through the rest with your attorney's input.

Step 6: Do NOT sign before you have the arrears number.

This is absolute. Article 9 says "preliminary outstanding balance." That word "preliminary" means you'd be agreeing to an undefined debt. With the pattern of overcharges — the acreage, the fuel, the O/F3M fields, the capital repairs — you cannot sign a lease that leaves the number open. Get the line-item accounting. Verify every charge against the spreadsheet. Apply your credits ($6,455 documented). Agree on a final number. Both parties sign it. Then — and only then — does it go into the lease.

Step 7: Keep farming.

While the lease is being negotiated, keep doing what you do. Plant your crops. Serve your customers. Cover-crop Don's fallow fields. Be the farmer the recitals describe. The strongest position in any negotiation is being the person who shows up and does the work. Don knows you do that. His customers know you do that. Your CSA members know you do that. That is worth more than any clause in any lease.

What I would NOT do:

  • I would NOT deliver a heavily marked-up lease without a lawyer's review first. Your analysis is thorough, but a lawyer will catch things we haven't — especially around the acknowledgment clauses in 4.4 and 4.5 and the indemnification scope in Article 11.

  • I would NOT threaten to leave or use the word "unfair." Frame everything as wanting to understand, wanting to make it work, wanting to honor the legacy. You catch more flies with honey — but bring the vinegar in your back pocket.
