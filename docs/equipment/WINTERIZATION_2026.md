# Putting machines away for winter — 2026

Applies to every gas engine on the farm, not just the two being worked on.
Written 2026-09-24.

**The one that matters most: fuel.** Nearly every "it won't start in spring"
call is fuel that sat. The mower is down right now *because* fuel lines rotted.
Ethanol gas absorbs water, separates, and varnishes a carburetor in a single
winter. Everything below is downstream of getting fuel right.

---

## Pick ONE fuel strategy per machine — do not half-do both

### Strategy A — Store it DRY (best for machines that will sit ≥4 months)
1. Run the tank as low as you can on the last use.
2. Drain the remaining fuel from the tank into a labeled can.
3. Drain the carburetor bowl — most have a drain screw on the bottom of the
   bowl. If not, pull the bowl.
4. Start it and let it run until it dies of fuel starvation. Pull the fuel line
   at the carb and crank a few seconds to clear the pump if fitted.
5. Leave the tank cap loose or off in a dry building so condensation can escape.

Dry storage is the only approach that cannot varnish a carburetor, because
there is nothing left in it to varnish.

### Strategy B — Store it FULL and STABILIZED (fine for 2–3 months)
1. Fill the tank to about 95% — full leaves no air for condensation, but leave
   room for expansion.
2. Add stabilizer at the dose on the bottle. **Use marine-grade / ethanol-rated
   stabilizer**, not the cheap red stuff, if there is any ethanol in the fuel.
3. **Run the engine 10–15 minutes after adding it.** This is the step everyone
   skips and it is the whole point — untreated fuel is still sitting in the
   carburetor bowl, float needle, and jets. Stabilizer in the tank protects
   nothing until it has been pulled through the carb.

**Best of all: buy ethanol-free gas for the last fill of the season.** It is
worth the premium on machines that sit. In western PA, ethanol-free is sold at
some marinas and a few stations — worth finding one and making it the standing
end-of-season fill.

## Everything else, in order

### 1. Change the oil while the engine is WARM
Do this before storage, not in spring. Used oil holds combustion acids and
moisture; leaving it in all winter etches bearings and cylinder walls. Warm oil
carries suspended contaminants out with it instead of leaving them in the pan.

Run 5 minutes, shut down, drain, replace the filter, refill to spec, run one
minute, recheck the level cold.

### 2. Grease every zerk
Fresh grease pushes water out of the joint. Water in a bearing over a PA winter
means a seized bearing in April. Hit every fitting until you see clean grease
move.

### 3. Fog the cylinder (10 minutes, prevents the expensive failure)
A bare cylinder wall rusts over winter, and rust rings are an engine rebuild.
- Pull the spark plug.
- Spray fogging oil into the plug hole for 3–5 seconds. (No fogging oil? A
  tablespoon of clean engine oil down the hole works.)
- Pull the recoil or bump the starter a few turns to spread it on the wall.
- Reinstall the plug — **new plug if you are replacing it anyway**.
- Leave the piston at top dead center: pull the plug wire and turn the engine
  until you feel compression resistance, then stop. Both valves are closed
  there, sealing the chamber against moist air.

### 4. Battery
Pull it, or put it on a maintainer. A battery left in an unheated building at
partial charge will freeze and be scrap by March. If you pull it, store it
somewhere above freezing on a maintainer, not on a concrete floor.

### 5. Wash and dry — then grease bare metal
Wash the deck and undercarriage. **Then dry it and get it out of the sun.**
Wet grass clippings left on steel all winter hold moisture against the metal and
rot a mower deck from the inside. Wipe a film of grease or oil on bare cut
edges, blade bolts, and any place paint is gone.

### 6. Tires
Set to spec pressure. Low tires sitting all winter flat-spot and crack at the
sidewall. If the machine sits on a dirt or concrete floor, put boards or blocks
under the tires.

### 7. Rodents — the one people forget
A mouse nest in a muffler or air intake ruins a spring startup, and chewed
wiring harnesses are a miserable repair.
- Stuff a rag in the exhaust outlet and the air intake. **Hang a bright tag on
  the steering wheel listing what you plugged**, so nobody starts it with a rag
  in the muffler.
- Bait or trap around the machines, not in them.
- Peppermint oil on cotton balls in the engine bay is cheap and helps.

### 8. Write it down
Every machine gets a line in `MACHINE_REGISTRY.md`: date, what was done, parts
used, cost. Next September the plan starts from a record instead of memory.

---

## Order of operations

Do the repair work FIRST, then winterize. A machine that gets a new carburetor
in November should be run, tuned, and confirmed good *before* the fuel comes
back out of it for storage. Otherwise you find the problem in April.

For the two machines currently down:

1. **Mower** — fuel lines, carburetor, tune-up → run and confirm → winterize.
2. **Mule** — diagnose the power loss → repair → confirm under load, on the
   actual hill → winterize.

Do not winterize a machine you have not confirmed works. Storage hides problems
for five months.
