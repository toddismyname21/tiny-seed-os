# Simplicity mower — Kohler CV14S — carburetor, fuel system, end-of-season tune-up

Engine **CV14S**, **SPEC 14107**, serial 3012206931. 398 cc, 14 HP vertical
shaft, OHV, **hydraulic valve lifters**, full pressure lubrication, cast iron
cylinder liner. Built 2000.

**Why it is down:** the fuel lines dry-rotted and it quit. Todd is replacing the
carburetor on the theory that debris got into it.

---

## The thing to get right before you order anything

**If rotted line put debris into the carburetor, that same debris is in the tank
and the fuel filter too.** Bolting a new carburetor onto a dirty fuel system
buys you a few hours of running and then the new carburetor is contaminated and
you are back where you started, out the cost of the part.

Rubber fuel line does not fail cleanly. It sheds from the inside — fine black
crumbs and a gummy film that goes wherever fuel goes. So this is a **fuel system
job**, not a carburetor job:

1. Drain and **clean the tank**
2. **All** new fuel line, every run of it
3. New fuel filter
4. New carburetor
5. New tank cap if the vent is questionable

Do the whole thing once. Doing it in pieces is how this repeat.

---

## SPEC NOTE — order parts by SPEC, not model

Kohler carburetors are keyed to the **spec number**, not just the model. Two
CV14S engines with different specs take different carburetors.

**When you order or call a dealer, give them: `CV14S`, `SPEC 14107`,
`SERIAL 3012206931`.**

The verified numbers are at the bottom of this document, pulled from the OEM
diagram for this exact engine. Do not let anyone sell you a carburetor on
"CV14S" alone — make them confirm it against spec 14107.

## SAFETY — read once, then do it every time

- **Gasoline.** Work outside or with the door open. No smoking, no grinder, no
  work light with a hot bulb near the tank. Have a fire extinguisher within
  reach, not across the shop.
- **Disconnect the negative battery cable.** First thing, before any fuel work.
- **Pull the spark plug wire and ground it to the block.** A mower that turns
  over while your hands are near the blades is how people lose fingers.
- **Let the engine go cold.** Fuel on a hot exhaust manifold ignites.
- Have a proper fuel container and rags ready **before** you open anything.
- Old gas is waste. Do not pour it on the ground or down a drain.

## PART 1 — Fuel system replacement

### 1.1 Drain the old fuel
- Close the fuel shutoff valve if fitted.
- Run the tank out, or siphon into a labeled container.
- **Look at what comes out.** Water sits at the bottom, separate and clear.
  Rust is orange. Rotted rubber is fine black grit. What you see here tells you
  how bad the rest is.

### 1.2 Pull and clean the tank
- Remove the tank. Note or photograph how the lines route before you unhook
  anything — the routing matters and is easy to get wrong on reassembly.
- Rinse it. If there is rust or sediment, put a cup of clean gravel or a length
  of chain in with some solvent, shake hard, dump, repeat until it comes out
  clean.
- **Dry it completely.** Compressed air, or leave it in the sun. Any water left
  is water in your new carburetor.
- Clean or replace the pickup screen.
- Check the **cap vent**. Blow through it. If it will not pass air the tank
  pulls a vacuum and the engine starves — the same symptom that looks like a
  carburetor fault. Replace the cap if in doubt.

### 1.3 Replace ALL fuel line
- **Take a piece of the old line to the store and match it.** Do not trust a
  number from a website. You need the right **inside diameter**, and a hose
  **rated for ethanol** — ordinary rubber line is what rotted in the first
  place. SAE 30R7 or better, or marine-grade.
- Replace every run: tank → filter → pump (if fitted) → carburetor.
- Cut clean and square. Ragged ends leak and shed.
- Use proper fuel-line clamps. Worm-gear hose clamps cut into soft line; the
  spring or pinch style is correct.
- Leave enough slack that nothing is stretched, and route away from the exhaust.
  **A fuel line touching a muffler is a fire.**

### 1.4 New inline fuel filter
- Install it with the **arrow pointing toward the carburetor**. Backwards, it
  restricts flow and starves the engine.
- Put it where you can see it — a clear filter lets you spot debris or water
  before it becomes a repair.

## PART 2 — Carburetor replacement

### 2.1 Before you remove the old one
**Photograph everything.** Every linkage, every spring, every hole a spring sits
in. Governor and throttle springs go in specific holes and the engine will run
badly or overspeed if they go back wrong. Take more pictures than you think you
need — this is the single most common way this job goes sideways.

### 2.2 Remove
- Air cleaner assembly off.
- Unhook the fuel line at the carb. Have a rag ready.
- Unhook governor linkage, throttle and choke linkage. **Note which hole each
  spring came from.**
- Remove the mounting bolts. Keep the old gaskets to compare against the new
  ones.

### 2.3 Inspect while it is apart — this is free information
- **Cut the old carburetor's bowl off and look inside.** If it is full of black
  grit, you have confirmed the debris theory and you know the tank cleaning was
  necessary. If it is clean, the problem may have been fuel starvation from the
  rotted line collapsing, not contamination.
- Look at the **intake manifold** and the gasket surface for cracks or a loose
  manifold — an air leak there causes a lean run and is easy to miss.
- Clean the mating surfaces. Old gasket material must come off completely.

### 2.4 Install
- **New gaskets. Always.** Never reuse a carburetor gasket.
- Get the gasket orientation right — some have a specific up side, and a
  misaligned one blocks a passage.
- Snug the mounting bolts **evenly, alternating**, to the manual's torque. These
  bolts thread into aluminum: overtightening strips the casting and turns a
  cheap job into an expensive one. If you do not have the spec, snug plus a
  small turn — firm, not gorilla.
- Reconnect linkages **to the photographed holes**.
- Reconnect fuel line, new clamps.

### 2.5 First start
- Battery back on, plug wire back on.
- Fuel valve open. Give it a minute to fill the bowl.
- **Check for leaks before you start it.** Look at every joint with a flashlight.
- Start it. Expect some stumbling while air clears.
- Let it warm, then check the idle and the throttle response. Confirm the engine
  reaches full governed speed and returns to a stable idle.
- **Shut it off and check for leaks again, warm.** Things that seal cold
  sometimes weep warm.

## PART 3 — End-of-season tune-up

### 3.1 Oil and filter — do it WARM
Run 5 minutes first. Warm oil carries the contaminants out instead of leaving
them in the pan.
- Drain fully. Replace the oil filter.
- Refill to the capacity in the manual with the specified viscosity. This engine
  has **full pressure lubrication and an oil filter** — it is not a splash-lube
  engine and the filter is not optional.
- Run one minute, shut off, wait, **recheck the level cold** and top up. New
  filters absorb oil and the level always drops.

### 3.2 Air filter AND pre-cleaner
Two parts. People replace the paper element and forget the foam pre-cleaner
wrapped around it.
- New paper element. **Do not blow it out with compressed air** — that tears the
  media and lets grit into the engine. If it is dirty, replace it.
- Pre-cleaner: wash in soapy water, dry completely, and lightly oil it if the
  manual calls for that. A dry pre-cleaner passes dust; a soaked one starves the
  engine.
- Check that the housing seals. A gap there makes the filter pointless.

### 3.3 Spark plug
- New plug, **gapped to spec** with a feeler gauge. Do not assume it is right
  out of the box.
- Read the old one before you throw it out — tan is good, black is rich, white
  is lean, oily means rings or guides.
- Torque to spec. It threads into aluminum.

### 3.4 Valves — nothing to do
This engine has **hydraulic valve lifters** (it is printed on the engine's own
decal). They self-adjust. **There is no valve lash procedure on this engine.**
If someone tells you to set the lash on a CV14S, they are thinking of a
different engine.

### 3.5 Cooling fins — the one that kills these engines
Air-cooled engines die from heat, and heat comes from packed debris.
- Pull the blower housing off. Not "look through the screen" — take it off.
- Clear every fin with compressed air and a brush. Chaff, grass, and mouse
  bedding pack in there solid and it is invisible with the shroud on.
- Clean the intake screen on the flywheel.

This is ten minutes and it is the difference between this engine lasting another
ten years or cooking a head.

### 3.6 Belts, blades, deck
- Inspect the drive and deck belts for glazing and cracks.
- Sharpen or replace the blades; check the spindle bearings for play by rocking
  each blade tip.
- Grease every zerk.
- Scrape the deck underside and check for rust-through.

### 3.7 Battery and electrical
- Clean terminals, check the charge.
- Check the safety interlocks actually work — seat switch, PTO, brake. They are
  there for a reason and they fail with age.

### 3.8 Then winterize
Follow `WINTERIZATION_2026.md`. **Run and confirm the repair first.** Do not put
a freshly rebuilt fuel system away untested and find out in April.

### Two things to do because it is aftermarket

1. **Buy the OEM gaskets separately — $5.48 total.** Carburetor gasket
   `12 041 01-S` ($2.49) and air cleaner base gasket `12 041 02-S` ($2.99).
   Aftermarket carbs ship with gaskets of unknown quality, and a gasket is the
   one part where failure means an air leak and a lean run you will chase for
   weeks. Cheapest insurance on this whole job.

2. **Keep the old carburetor.** Do not throw it out after you cut the bowl open.
   These aftermarket carbs advertise fitment across **CV12.5 through CV16** —
   12.5 to 16 HP on one part number. Real jetting is not identical across that
   range. If the new one runs rich or lean, the old carb is your reference for
   comparing jet sizes, and its float and needle are known-correct for spec
   14107.

**The honest risk:** a "fits everything" carburetor may be jetted for the middle
of that range rather than for your engine. Most people never notice. If yours
runs black-smoky or surges at part throttle after the swap, that is what
happened — and it is usually correctable with the mixture screw or by swapping
the main jet from the old carb.

## Also before you order

### Does yours have a fuel PUMP or is it GRAVITY feed?
The diagram lists both a fuel pump kit (12 559 02-S) **and** a filter labeled
"gravity feed" (25 050 21-S). This engine family was built both ways.

**Go look before you order.** Follow the line from the tank. If there is a pump
on the engine, its diaphragm is the same age as the rotted hose and should be
replaced at the same time. If it is gravity feed, skip the pump entirely.

### Three parts are superseded
Order the **new** numbers, not the ones cast into the old parts:
`25 237 08-S → 25 237 37-S` · `25 111 34-S → 25 111 81-S` ·
`25 237 14-S → 25 237 31-S`
