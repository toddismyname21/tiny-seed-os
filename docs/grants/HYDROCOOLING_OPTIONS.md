# Hydrocooling — the options, and what the research actually says

**Built 2026-10-02.** Todd: *"the cooling could change depending on the day and
capacity. Hydro cooling. What are our options there?"*

Everything below was read today from **Penn State Extension, "Keeping Produce
Fresh: Best Practices for Producers"** (updated 2017-07-31),
`extension.psu.edu/keeping-produce-fresh-best-practices-for-producers`, which
cites Kitinoja and Thompson (2010). Several of the photos in that article are
from **Spiral Path Farm in Pennsylvania**, so the examples are regional.

---

# 1. ⭐ THE FINDING THAT SETTLES THE FORCED-AIR QUESTION

Penn State's **Table 5** lists the preferred cooling method per crop.
`F = forced-air · H = hydrocooling · I = icing · R = room · V = vacuum`

| Crop | Preferred method |
|---|---|
| **Lettuce** | **H, I** |
| **Spinach** | **H, I** |
| **Tomatoes** | **R, F** |

**Read that twice.** Our two grant commodities want *opposite* cooling methods.

- **Salad greens → hydrocooling or icing.** Forced-air is not even listed.
- **Tomatoes → room or forced-air.** Hydrocooling is not listed, and immersing
  tomatoes in colder water risks drawing water in through the stem scar.

✅ **So the $835.52 forced-air line is NOT wasted — it was justified under the
wrong commodity.** It is the correct tool for **tomatoes**, which is commodity #2
on the PASA application. It should be re-described that way.

🔴 **And greens have no cooling step at all right now.** That is the real gap.

---

# 2. WHY THIS MATTERS MORE FOR GREENS THAN ANYTHING ELSE

Direct quotes from the article:

> *"A one-hour delay in cooling can reduce the shelf life of produce by a day or
> more."*

> *"Prompt cooling is especially critical for crops with high respiration rates
> and high surface area. For example, the respiration rate of salad greens at
> 50°F is **four times higher** than it is at 32°F."*

> *"On this moderate summer day with a high of 85°F at 8:00 a.m., the produce
> temperature was 61°F. At 11:00 a.m., it was **74°F**."*

Baby greens are the worst case in the whole catalogue — enormous surface area per
pound, high respiration, and they wilt. **Shelf life is the entire wholesale
argument**, and it is set in the first hour.

---

# 3. THE METHOD COMPARISON — Penn State Table 6

| Factor | Room | Forced-air | **Hydrocooling** | Package Ice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Typical cooling time (hours)** | 20–100 | 1–10 | **0.1–1.0** | 0.1–0.3 |
| **Produce moisture loss (%)** | 0.1–2.0 | 0.1–2.0 | **0–0.5** | no data |
| Water contact with produce | No | No | **Yes** | Yes |
| **Potential for decay contamination** | Low | Low | 🔴 **HIGH** | Low |
| **Capital cost** | Low–medium | Low | **Low** | High |
| Portability | No | Sometimes | Rare | Yes |

**Hydrocooling is 10–100× faster than forced air and loses the least moisture** —
moisture loss being exactly what makes greens limp. It is also **low capital.**

🔴 **The one real cost is food safety.** *"Due to food safety concerns,
hydrocooling is becoming less widely used because of the potential for water to
move pathogens among produce."* Water touching every leaf means one contaminated
batch can reach the whole tank.

> ⚠️ **But note: the farm already does this.** Greens already go into a wash
> tank. **The water-contact risk is already being run every single day.** Adding
> ice to that water does not introduce a new hazard category — it converts an
> existing step into a cooling step. What it *does* demand is that the water
> management get serious, which is overdue regardless.

## Option C — ⭐ Used stainless bulk farm milk cooler.

Penn State recommends this directly:

> *"Used stainless steel bulk farm milk coolers may be an option."*
> *"Large bulk tanks can be used to hydrocool produce."*

**Why it is clever:** a bulk milk tank is already food-grade stainless, already
insulated, already has **built-in mechanical refrigeration**, and usually has an
**agitator** — which is most of a purpose-built hydrocooler for scrap value.
Dairy country in western PA means these turn up at farm auctions and on
Marketplace, the same channel that found the bagging table and the flail.

**Price: UNVERIFIED.** Varies enormously by size and condition. Worth watching
for.

## Option D — Fabricated thermal-storage immersion hydrocooler.

> *"Mechanical refrigeration is the most efficient method for cooling water. A
> thermal storage immersion hydrocooler system can be fabricated economically to
> suit various volume requirements."*

Build ice overnight on off-peak power, dump the stored cold into the tank during
the harvest rush. Matches the farm's existing build-it approach (the tri-clamp
manifold, the forced-air coolers). **Cost UNVERIFIED — needs a design first.**

## Option E — Well water. **Insufficient alone.**

> *"Most well water is 55 to 60°F and will not bring all produce down to optimum
> temperature… you will need to create a multistep system that cools the rest of
> the way with room or forced-air cooling."*

Useful as a **first stage** — free, and it takes the top off field heat before
the ice does the rest. Not a cooling system by itself.

## Option F — Shower / spray hydrocooler. ❌ **Wrong for baby greens.**

> *"Water pressure can cause surface pitting and water-soaked damage on sensitive
> leaves and fruit."*

Also needs *"about 10 gallons per minute per square foot of cooling area"* with
nozzles kept within 6–8 inches. **Immersion, not spray, for baby leaf.**

# 4b. ⭐ "HOW DO WE GET THE ICE OUT OF THE GREENS?" — keep them apart

**Todd, 2026-10-02:** *"i dont understand how we get the ice out of the greens
when spinning dry. unless the water circulates through the ice."*

**That second sentence is the answer.** Nobody dumps loose cubes in with the
product and then tries to fish them out. **The ice chills the water; the water
chills the greens; the ice and the greens never meet.** This is exactly what
Penn State means by a *"thermal storage immersion hydrocooler."*

Three ways to do it, cheapest first.

## 4b.1 — Mesh bags of ice. **$0–20. Do this tomorrow.**

Ice in mesh/laundry bags, hung or floated at one end of the tank. Lift them out
before the greens go in, or leave them in and skim around them.

✅ Costs nothing, works today, proves the whole idea before any money is spent.
❌ Fiddly, and the bags sit where you want to be working.

## 4b.2 — ⭐ Perforated ice cage + the bubbler already being built. **~$100–200.**

A perforated stainless basket or cage at one end of the 120" tank, holding the
ice. **The spa-blower manifold already on order does the circulating** — the
agitation that scrubs the greens also drives tank water through the ice cage and
back.

⭐ **This is the one to build, because it costs almost nothing on top of work
already planned.** The tri-clamp manifold was specified to wash. The same
airflow, with a cage at one end, makes it wash *and* chill. One tank, one blower,
two jobs.

| Why it fits | |
|---|---|
| Circulation | already provided by the spa blower + manifold |
| Separation | the cage — greens physically cannot reach the ice |
| Cleanability | perforated stainless, same food-grade standard as the manifold |
| Cost | a stainless cage and a mount |

## 4b.3 — Separate ice reservoir + recirculating pump. **More plumbing.**

Ice and water in a second insulated tank. A pump sends chilled water to the wash
tank; the overflow returns to the ice tank. Full separation, and the cold is
stored where it is not in the way.

**This is where a used stainless bulk milk tank earns its keep** — insulated,
food-grade, already has refrigeration and an agitator.

❌ More pump, plumbing, and sanitation surface than 4b.2. Worth it only at a
volume the tank-and-cage cannot keep up with.

## The point that makes all of this easy

**Cold water does the work, not cold ice.** Ice is only a way of *storing* cold
cheaply. Once that is clear, the design question stops being "how do I separate
ice from greens" and becomes "where do I keep the ice so the water can run past
it" — and the answer is: at the far end of the tank, in a cage, with the blower
already pushing water through it.

> ⚠️ **Consequence for the dry side:** nothing changes. Greens come out of a cold
> tank wet and cold, go into the spinner exactly as they do now, and come out
> cold and dry. **No ice ever enters the spinner.** The restored Mannhart and
> Greens Machine baskets are unaffected.

# 5. WHAT PENN STATE SAYS THE TARGET IS

> *"In this hydrocooling system, lettuce on a conveyor goes through chilled
> (**34°F**) water in order to quickly cool."* — Spiral Path Farm

And the two-stage pattern, from the same article's own photo captions:

| Stage | Method |
|---|---|
| 1. Remove field heat | **hydrocool / ice water** |
| 2. Hold the clean product | *"Clean baby greens are cooled by **forced-air cooling** inside a larger walk-in"* — Spiral Path Farm |

**So it is not either/or.** The PA farm Penn State photographed does *both* —
hydrocool to strip field heat, then forced-air in the walk-in to hold. That is
the model to copy.

## Why this is a strong grant line rather than padding

It is not new capability for its own sake. **Greens currently get no cooling
step** — they sit in a stock tank at ambient while being bagged three touches at
a time, and Penn State says each hour of that costs a day of shelf life. Shelf
life is the thing wholesale buyers actually buy. The ice machine ties to a
specific commodity (salad greens), a specific FSA field, and the conservation
practice, and at $2,529 it is comfortably under the $10,000 per-item cap.

**And it is the rare line that pays for itself.** Bagged ice at $10 a bag costs
the farm $2,600–$5,200 every season. The machine costs $2,529 once. A reviewer
asking "does this expand market access or just spend money" has an arithmetic
answer.
