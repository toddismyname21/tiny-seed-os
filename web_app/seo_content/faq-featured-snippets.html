CSA Basics

What is a CSA?

CSA stands for Community Supported Agriculture. It's a partnership between a local farm and community members who purchase a "share" of the farm's harvest.

Here's how it works: Members pay upfront or in installments at the beginning of the growing season, and in return receive weekly boxes of fresh, seasonal vegetables throughout the harvest period (typically 20-24 weeks from late spring through fall).

CSA connects consumers directly with local farmers, providing the freshest possible produce while supporting sustainable local agriculture. When you join a CSA, you're not just buying vegetables - you're investing in a local farm and becoming part of an agricultural community.

What does CSA stand for?

CSA stands for Community Supported Agriculture.

The name reflects the core concept: community members directly support a local farm by purchasing shares of the harvest in advance. This model originated in Japan in the 1960s (where it was called "teikei") and came to the United States in the mid-1980s.

The "community" aspect emphasizes the partnership between farmers and consumers, sharing both the bounty and the risks of farming together.

How does a CSA work?

A CSA works through a seasonal membership model:

  • Sign up before the growing season begins (typically January-April) and choose your share size
  • Pay for your membership upfront or through a payment plan
  • Receive weekly boxes of fresh vegetables starting in late spring, either through home delivery or pickup
  • Each week's box contains whatever is ripe and ready on the farm that week
  • Season runs approximately 20-24 weeks through fall

Some CSAs allow customization, skip weeks for vacations, or offer add-ons like eggs, meat, or flowers.

Cost & Value

How much does a CSA cost in Pittsburgh?

Pittsburgh CSA memberships typically cost between $25-45 per week depending on box size and farm:

  • Small share (1-2 people): $25-30/week
  • Standard share (2-4 people): $30-40/week
  • Family share (4+ people): $40-55/week

For a full season (20-24 weeks), total costs range from $500-$1,200.

Many Pittsburgh farms offer payment plans, early-bird discounts, and working member options to reduce costs. Delivery is often included in the price with no additional fees.

Is a CSA worth it?

A CSA is worth it for most families who value fresh, local produce and want to support local agriculture.

Benefits that make CSA worthwhile:

  • Produce significantly fresher than grocery stores (often harvested within 24 hours)
  • Higher nutritional value due to freshness
  • Exposure to new seasonal vegetables
  • Direct relationship with your farmer
  • Supporting sustainable local farming

Cost-wise, CSA produce typically equals or beats organic grocery store prices while being far fresher.

The main consideration is committing to eating seasonally and being flexible with what you receive each week - but most members find this becomes one of their favorite aspects.

Timing & Seasons

When does CSA season start in Pittsburgh?

CSA season in Pittsburgh typically starts in late May or early June and runs through October or November, spanning approximately 20-24 weeks.

The exact start date depends on spring weather and growing conditions each year.

Important timing:

  • CSA registration usually opens in January through March
  • Popular farms often sell out by April
  • Some farms offer winter shares with storage crops
  • Year-round options are available from select farms

We recommend signing up early to secure your spot, especially for popular share sizes.

How long does CSA produce last?

CSA produce typically lasts longer than grocery store produce because it's fresher when you receive it.

General storage guidelines:

  • Leafy greens: 5-7 days in refrigerator
  • Tomatoes: 5-7 days at room temperature
  • Peppers and cucumbers: 1-2 weeks in refrigerator
  • Root vegetables (carrots, beets, potatoes): 2-4 weeks in cool storage
  • Winter squash: 1-3 months in cool, dry place
  • Onions and garlic: 1-2 months

Proper storage extends produce life significantly. Most farms provide storage tips with each delivery.

What's Included

What vegetables are in a CSA box?

CSA box contents vary by season and typically include 8-12 different vegetables weekly.

Spring boxes (May-June): Lettuce, spinach, radishes, spring onions, herbs, peas, asparagus

Summer boxes (July-August): Tomatoes (multiple varieties), corn, peppers, zucchini, cucumbers, green beans, eggplant, basil

Fall boxes (September-October): Winter squash, potatoes, carrots, beets, kale, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, onions

A standard weekly share contains approximately 10-15 pounds of produce, which feeds 2-4 people depending on how much produce your family eats.

Can I customize my CSA box?

Customization options vary by CSA:

  • Traditional CSAs provide a set box based on what's harvested that week
  • Direct farm CSAs like Tiny Seed Farm often work with members on preferences and dietary restrictions - if you can't eat certain vegetables, we make a note
  • Some CSAs offer swap options or a la carte add-ons

For the best customization with a direct farm, simply communicate your needs to your farmer. We remember that your family doesn't eat beets or that you want extra tomatoes when available.

What if I don't like a vegetable in my CSA?

If you receive vegetables you don't like or know how to cook:

  • Tell your farmer - many CSAs accommodate preferences and can note dislikes for future boxes
  • Try new recipes - farms often provide preparation suggestions
  • Share with neighbors or friends
  • Use it as an opportunity to expand your palate

Most CSA members discover new favorite vegetables they never would have bought at a store. The seasonal variety encourages diverse, healthy eating and culinary adventure.

Delivery & Logistics

Can I skip weeks with a CSA?

Most CSAs allow members to skip weeks for vacations or other conflicts.

Policies vary by farm:

  • Some farms let you pause and extend your membership by skipped weeks
  • Others may offer credits for future use
  • Some allow you to donate your box to a food bank when you're away

You typically need to notify the farm in advance - usually by the weekend before your scheduled delivery.

At Tiny Seed Farm, we offer flexible skip options because we know life happens. Just let us know and we'll adjust your delivery schedule.

What happens if I'm not home for CSA delivery?

If you're not home for CSA delivery, most farms leave the box in a designated spot you specify - typically your porch, doorstep, or building entrance.

Best practices:

  • Provide delivery instructions when you sign up
  • Designate a shaded spot in summer
  • Have a cooler available for hot days
  • Arrange delivery to a neighbor who's home if needed

CSA boxes are designed to keep produce fresh for several hours. You'll usually receive a notification when your box is delivered. Most farms are flexible and work with members on delivery logistics.

CSA vs. Other Options

What is the difference between a CSA and a produce delivery service?

The key differences between a CSA and produce delivery services:

  • Source: CSAs come directly from one local farm; delivery services source from multiple places including wholesalers
  • Freshness: CSA produce is harvested 24-48 hours before delivery; delivery services may be days older
  • Relationship: CSA members know their farmer personally
  • Seasonality: CSAs reflect what's actually growing locally
  • Economic impact: CSA money goes directly to farmers; delivery services take significant percentages

CSAs emphasize connection and freshness; delivery services prioritize convenience and year-round variety. For maximum freshness and to support local farms directly, a CSA is the better choice.

How do I find a CSA near me in Pittsburgh?

To find a CSA in Pittsburgh:

  • Search "CSA Pittsburgh" or "Pittsburgh farm share" online
  • Check LocalHarvest.org for farms in your area
  • Visit Pittsburgh-area farmers markets and ask vendors about CSA
  • Ask neighbors and friends for recommendations
  • Contact farms directly

When evaluating options, consider: delivery vs. pickup, farm distance and freshness, share sizes offered, cost, and whether you can visit the farm or speak directly with the farmer.
